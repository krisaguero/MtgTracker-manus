# The Precon Swerve: Build a Foundations Precon Cube Draft

*How to turn Foundations Commander decks into a replayable, narrative-driven limited experience without confusing a house format with official Commander Draft.*

The traditional playbook for a Magic collector is simple: buy a Commander precon, sleeve it, put it on a shelf, and wait for the product to become interesting again. That works for sealed collecting, but it leaves a lot of the best part unused. A precon is not only a sealed object. It is also a curated pool of commanders, themes, mana, interaction, tokens, and stories that can be recombined at the table.

The **Precon Cube Draft** is a way to use that pool repeatedly. You take cards from several compatible preconstructed decks, reorganize them into a custom limited environment, and draft a new deck from the collection. Each session creates a different version of the same setting. A dragon deck can become a graveyard deck. An artifact deck can borrow a sacrifice engine. A pile of mono-colored beginner products can become a small, replayable game-night system.

This is not an official Wizards format. It is a house-rule experience inspired by Commander Draft and Commander Box League. That distinction matters, because the official formats use different products and deck-building rules.

## Start with the right Foundations pool

Foundations Commander includes five mono-colored, ready-to-play 100-card decks: **Calling All Angels**, **Keen Engineering**, **Wretched Ranks**, **Reign of Dragons**, and **Tramplesaurus Rex**. Wizards describes them as straightforward decks intended to introduce players to Commander, with each deck built around a clear theme such as artifacts, Dragons, or large creatures. Each product includes a 100-card Commander deck, a traditional foil borderless face commander, tokens, a reference card, and a deck box. [1]

That makes the product line a useful starting point for a cube. The decks are thematically legible, but their cards are not locked into one permanent identity. The mono-colored structure also gives you a clean way to decide which cards are foundational, which cards are theme payoffs, and which cards can be moved into a shared pool.

A first cube does not need all five decks. Two or three decks can make a focused environment. **Reign of Dragons** and **Wretched Ranks** create the most obvious narrative collision: Dragons, Treasure, Zombies, sacrifice, and graveyard recursion. **Keen Engineering** adds an artifact lane that can connect to either deck through sacrifice, token production, and recursion. **Calling All Angels** and **Tramplesaurus Rex** create a larger-creature environment where combat and board presence matter more than stack interaction.

Keep the original deck boxes and a complete inventory record. The cube is a play configuration, not permission to forget where each physical card came from.

## Official Commander Draft versus your house cube

Official Commander Draft is a limited format supported by Wizards. Players open three booster packs and draft two cards at a time. The format is designed for groups of four, uses 40 starting life, allows multiple copies of cards drafted by a player, and produces decks of at least 60 cards. Wizards lists Commander Legends, Commander Legends: Battle for Baldur’s Gate, and Commander Masters as products designed for Commander Draft. [2]

Your Precon Cube Draft is different. The cards come from your own precons, the packs are custom, and the deck-building rules are choices your group makes before starting. Calling the experience a “Commander Draft” without explaining those differences can make the format sound official when it is not.

A practical house ruleset looks like this:

- Break down cards from two to five Foundations Commander decks and create custom 20-card packs.
- Draft one pack at a time, passing it around the table, or use a sealed pool if you prefer a faster setup.
- Build a 60-card singleton deck from the cards you drafted, including a commander from your pool.
- Use normal Commander color identity unless the group agrees to a special limited rule.
- Use basic lands from a shared land station rather than spending draft picks on every land.
- Allow a short between-round rebuild using only the drafted pool.

The singleton rule is an intentional design choice here. Official Commander Draft permits multiple copies of cards drafted by a player, while this house version can preserve the familiar singleton identity of Commander. If a group wants the official limited feel, allow duplicates. If the group wants every deck to feel like a Commander deck, keep singleton. Both are valid; they are simply different games.

## Step 1: Open the collection, not the story

The goal is not to destroy the value of a sealed product for its own sake. The goal is to decide what kind of experience you want the collection to provide.

Before sorting, record each deck’s sealed ownership separately from its opened-card inventory. Then mark which cards are entering the cube. A card inside the cube should not simultaneously be counted as available for a finished deck, a transfer package, or a purchase plan. This is where collection-aware tracking matters: the cube is a real reservation of physical cards.

For the first session, use a limited selection rather than every card. A manageable cube might contain roughly 160 to 240 cards, plus basic lands and tokens. Select enough ramp, removal, card draw, creatures, and mana fixing that players can build functioning decks. Do not include only the flashy tribal payoffs. A cube full of seven-mana Dragons and Zombie lords will produce exciting reveals but awkward games.

The strongest first version usually includes:

- A recognizable group of commanders and legendary creatures.
- Cheap creatures and spells that establish each theme.
- A few broad answers that can interact with different permanent types.
- Enough mana rocks, fixing, and basic lands to make splashes possible.
- Token cards and reminder cards stored with the cube.

## Step 2: Rebuild the narrative archetypes

A precon has a planned identity. A draft pool creates collisions between identities. That collision is the point.

### Necromantic Dragons

Combine the Dragon and Zombie pools from **Reign of Dragons** and **Wretched Ranks**. A player who opens enough Dragons may still want black cards that bring creatures back, sacrifice creatures for value, or reward a full graveyard. The result is no longer a straightforward Dragon deck. It becomes a story about apex creatures that refuse to stay dead.

The important design choice is to reward the splash without making it automatic. Include enough black reanimation and sacrifice effects to make the plan plausible, but not so many that every draft becomes the same deck. Treasure from the Dragon pool can help support the splash, while Zombie token production gives the graveyard player a way to survive until the larger threats arrive.

### Artifact Expedition

Use **Keen Engineering** as the mechanical bridge. Thopter production, artifact creatures, sacrifice outlets, and cost reduction can connect to almost any other pool. Wretched Ranks contributes death and recursion themes. Reign of Dragons contributes Treasure and large artifact-compatible threats. The resulting deck may be a small artifact engine, a sacrifice deck with flying tokens, or a value deck that turns cheap artifacts into a board.

This archetype is particularly good for a collection because its cards can move between multiple finished Commander decks. A card that is not needed in the cube can return to Keen Engineering or another artifact build without becoming dead inventory.

### Angels and Beasts

Combine **Calling All Angels** and **Tramplesaurus Rex** for a creature-focused environment. Angels supply evasive threats and stabilizing effects. Beasts and Dinosaurs supply power, trample, and combat pressure. The draft question becomes whether to stay in a clean creature lane or splash a second color for removal, ramp, or a graveyard package.

This version is easier to teach because the game plan is visible. Play creatures, attack, protect the best threat, and decide whether the table is asking for a wide board or one enormous finisher.

## Step 3: Curate for replayability

A good cube is not a pile of every card you own. It is a small environment with enough overlap to support multiple decks and enough tension to make players choose.

If one commander or one tribal payoff appears in every successful deck, reduce its support or move it into a special “featured card” box. If a color cannot build a functional deck without borrowing half of another color’s pool, add more fixing or simplify the intended archetype. If every game ends with the same expensive finisher, add alternate win conditions and more interactive answers.

Keep a change log after each draft night. Record which cards were never drafted, which cards were fought over, which archetypes failed to assemble, and which cards created repetitive games. Then adjust the pool in small increments. A cube becomes better through observed play, not through adding every upgrade recommendation at once.

The tracker can treat this as a project with three layers:

1. **Source inventory:** which opened precon or collection entry owns the physical copy.
2. **Cube reservation:** which copies are currently committed to the draft environment.
3. **Draft history:** which cards and commanders were used in each session.

That separation prevents a common collection error: counting a card as available in three places because each decklist is theoretically allowed to use it.

## Step 4: Make the experience feel finished

The easiest way to make the cube feel like a real product is to give it a clear physical home. Use a labeled storage box with dividers for commanders, creatures, interaction, mana, tokens, and basic lands. Keep the custom 20-card packs together if you want a repeatable sealed experience, or randomize from the full pool before each draft if you want a more unpredictable night.

Write a short introduction card for the box. It should explain that this is a custom Precon Cube Draft, identify the participating Foundations decks, state the deck size and singleton rule, and remind players that basic lands come from the shared station. A rules card eliminates the most common argument before the first pick.

Consider creating two modes:

- **Story mode:** preserve each deck’s themes and encourage narrative archetypes.
- **Chaos mode:** mix the cards more aggressively and let commanders define the deck after the draft.

Story mode is better for a new group. Chaos mode is better once the players know the pool and want to discover combinations that were not obvious from the packaging.

## The collector’s real return

The financial value of a precon can change with reprints, supply, condition, and demand. A cube does not protect that value, and it should not be presented as an investment strategy. What it can protect is the usefulness of the collection.

A sealed deck that never leaves the shelf has one primary experience attached to it. A curated cube has many. It can teach a new player, create a low-power night for an experienced group, test a commander before building the full deck, and reveal cross-deck relationships that would stay invisible in a static list.

That is the better reason to open the product. You are not abandoning collecting. You are giving the collection another job.

## Recommended first build

Start with **Reign of Dragons**, **Wretched Ranks**, and **Keen Engineering**. This combination gives the cube three distinct engines: large creatures and Treasure, sacrifice and graveyard value, and artifacts and tokens. Use a 180-card pool, create nine custom 20-card packs, use a shared basic-land station, and build 60-card singleton decks.

After two or three sessions, decide whether the cube needs the clean creature lane from Calling All Angels or the high-power combat lane from Tramplesaurus Rex. Add those decks only when you know what kind of games the first pool is failing to produce.

The most important rule is simple: the cube should make the group want to play again. If it does, your precon collection is no longer a shelf of fixed products. It is a reusable game system with a different story waiting every time the cards are shuffled.
